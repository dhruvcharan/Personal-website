# There's No There There: AI and the death of sincerity
# by Dhruv Charan
## Introduction

So there's this transformation that has been happening on the internet, and it has been bugging me, so I figured I would put pen to paper and get this article out as a means of venting this frustration. So much of what I come across on a bunch of these social media platforms feels so obviously written (or rather generated) to farm engagement by pandering to some or the other nebulous algorithmic recommendation system. As much as 54% of all LinkedIn posts appear to have been generated by AI tools and LLMS [1]. These posts, in turn, are full of people spouting vapid AI-generated platitudes in response as a means of farming some engagement of their own back, to min-max their standing in this attention economy. Non-human written content has increased by as much as 189% in just the last year on LinkedIn, and I am being purposefully focused on LinkedIn right now because it is the platform I am spending most of my time online on presently. So much of the internet, while more abuzz with activity than ever, feels lifeless and hollow. What happens to authenticity, connection, and trust when everything is constructed via synthetic means? What do you do when you realize that an idea you are engaging with never had a soul behind it? What if, as Gertrude Stein once said, "There's no there there"?

## The Algorithm Economy

Let's examine the core issue that is causing this. Much of everything these days is built to optimize for one thing over all else: eyeballs. No, that is not to say that the algorithms are harvesting human oculi, but rather the algorithms at the core of our social media feeds are instructed to maximize time spent on the platforms as a broader goal over much else. I am sure I am simplifying immensely when I say this, and that there are a plethora of nuances to the way the suite of machine learning and heuristic algorithms these platforms utilize to build the infinite scroll feeds we have grown so accustomed to. But for the sake of expounding my ideas, I must resort to some level of reduction, and I am sure I am not completely off the mark here in making this simplification. The vying for visibility almost pressures users to make sure they fit in the molds they presume these algorithms reward.

## The Flattening of Ideas

This phenomenon leads to the eventual flattening of ideas that I discussed in my previous article, where so much of what we see gets extruded through the die of what the creator thinks is likely to get picked up by the algorithm. We see this everywhere, from the sea of Mr Beast-style exaggerated and hyper-clickable thumbnails littering YouTube, to the same-ish posts on LinkedIn, or the broadly uniform aesthetic of various urban cafes [2]. On LinkedIn, this manifests itself as the sea of near-identical anecdotes about leadership, or resilience, or any of the other corporate banalities that the algorithm tends to reward. Because one thing that any Machine Learning model loves is predictability, and content that has worked in the near past has an almost assuredly higher chance of being rewarded by these algorithms than something novel. And that almost assuredly foments conformity over experimentation due to the fear of missing out on the engagement, particularly on LinkedIn, where that can probably be a precursor to a huge professional inflection point if you manage to capture the right kind of attention.

## Engagement Arms Race

And this was compounded by orders of magnitude when the tools for producing text that follows guidelines to a T and sounds plausible , and often smarter than you, while doing it became widely accessible. Not that it was a bad thing overall; AI has enabled incredible advances in enabling better understanding of the world around us, building individually catered learning tools for students, allowing everybody to have immediate, well-structured access to all the information in the world at an instant in a way that has never been possible, and increasing our productivity by a lot too. But just as we extol the virtues of this groundbreaking technology, so must we be mindful of the higher-order effects of it.

## The Productivity Paradox

Another reason why we might feel compelled to use LLMs to generate our content is that by not doing so, we might be leaving a more productive version of ourselves on the table, and so we are almost cajoled or lulled into maximizing our use of AI. Which I do not entirely agree with unless we define our conception of productivity as "Getting more things done in the shortest amount of time." This enabling of en masse content generation has given rise to an avalanche of what social media refers to as "slop", unoriginal content that is devoid of meaning or substance, meant only to be looked at once, not actually engaged with, because there's nothing behind it. And I want to explore what it means when there is no meaning to what we put out in the world, and maybe offer some pushback against some of these rationales.

The problem with the previously mentioned widget-style productivity definition of doing the most amount of "stuff" is that it seems to be entirely divorced from the craft of whatever it is doing. It is a rather bullet-pointsified version of what the work entails, and this holds for whatever kind of work we might want to replace with AI that is not entirely clinical and rote. If there is some work that you derive no joy or satisfaction from performing, sure, go ahead and let an AI do it for you entirely. But if it's something you feel is worth doing, I believe it's more than fair to be mindful of what it is that we are giving up. Because it seems to me that there is no long-term value-add from just shifting the cognitive responsibilities haphazardly, given that in doing so, we are not freeing up time for more virtuous undertakings but rather scrolling endlessly on apps that fragment our attention further. We surely need to have some arduous mental exercise, if nothing else, just to prevent the dulling of our brains.

## Knock Knock?

Another problem with building this is the absence of the humanity of it all. There is no acknowledgement in any of the AI-generated posts that are out there on LinkedIn of the fact that we are presenting versions of ourselves, crafted almost exclusively to appease mercurial and nebulous algorithms. There is something uncanny about realizing that the thing that we just read might not have come from a human being at all. To quote Marshall McLuhan, “The Medium is the Message”, and the only message I believe an autogenerated communication sends is one of disinterest.  It communicates a willing detachment, of not wishing to engage with a person or an idea so badly that we are glad to delegate doing so to a 3rd party. It is, in my view, just very disingenuous to pass the voice of an entity entirely disconnected from your lived experience as one of your own, and maybe most people would be comfortable with doing that, but I for one am not. And to reassert my older point, this also leads to creative and cultural stagnation and homogenization, as these algorithms start getting fed content driven by imitation, repetition and predictability and learn to reward it. This makes everything transactional, shallow, and driven purely by measurable outcomes instead of an authentic exchange of ideas. And what happens when we go one step further, using AI not only to do more, but in fact cheat?

## CAAS (Cheating as a service)

By now, you probably must have heard of Cluely(in fact, its launch was what drove me to write this piece in the first place), the AI service explicitly designed to, as stated on their website, “Cheat on everything”. It, in fact, encourages its users to “Start Cheating, because when everyone does, no one is”. And if that isn’t a perfect encapsulation of what lies ahead in the event we relinquish our morality in favor of getting more done quicker, I don’t know what is. To me this proclamation fills me with dread instead of hope because it signals that we might just be barreling towards a zero-trust society, one fueled by nihilism and a myopic “I’ve got mine” attitude, where immediate personal gain supersedes collective well-being and social mores, progressively eroding trust and mutual acknowledgement of fairness. And I am already seeing it in the current tech job market, where the strat seems to be cheating through all steps of the hiring cycle, from resumes generated entirely from scratch to complement a particular job description, to AI-assisted tools that will practically give the interview for you with the company at the other end being none the wiser [3]. And this has created an inherently broken space, where distrust and suspicion are the norm. And it disproportionately affects people like me who are holding steady and not succumbing to the tantalizing pull of taking shortcuts, who now find themselves at a tremendous disadvantage in a system that consistently rewards deception over genuineness. In game theory, this is a situation that eventually leads to the optimal strategy being mutual deception, where individual cooperation leads to the most suboptimal outcome for the individual, even when mutual cooperation would have yielded a higher payoff on the whole. A society like this is characterized by pervasive suspicion, dissolution of empathy and cooperation, and immense inefficiency. Where everybody is striving to find whatever advantage (however ill-got it may be) in this zero-sum game of them vs everybody. If left unchecked, this might be something that is in the cards for us, a situation where it is nearly impossible to distinguish between genuine insight and fabrication, which unsettles me greatly.



## Where do we go from here?

However, despite the bleakness of it all, there remains reason for cautious optimism. Because by shining light on and grappling with the consequences of our current trajectory, we might find solutions towards authenticity. Maybe the remedy lies in recognizing the fact that our imperfections, the rougher edges of our ideas and our quirks are what make us us. Qualities that cannot be sanded out by these tools unless we willingly give ourselves in. As long as sincerity, care, and thoughtful consideration remain valued over the relentless min-maxing of expediency, there remains hope that we can chisel ourselves free from a hollow, synthetic future and craft instead a reality shaped around the richness at the core of the human condition.
